SPACE

Clue to "Glenn Effect"
In Astronaut's Pocket

THE CLUE to the identity of the luminous particles observed by Astronaut John H. Glenn Jr. during his orbit in the pocket of the next orbiting astronaut.

A small pocket spectroscope carried along in space should solve the mystery of what the bright spots, known as the Glenn Effect, are, Dr. Charles M. Herzfeld of the Department of Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency said in Science 136:1121, 1962. Dr. Herzfeld believes that the color of the particles and their pattern of visibility indicate that they are condensed nitrogen and possibly oxygen irradiated by the sun.

Other scientists have theorized that the shiny spots may be flakes of ice or paint from the capsule. The yellowish-green color of the particles rules against the ice theory, Dr. Herzfeld told Science Service. "Ice crystals are colorless or white; luminous nitrogen is light yellow-green. He gives no support to the paint flake theory.

The ARPA research scientist believes that nitrogen and oxygen released by the capsule became luminous when the rays of the rising sun hit them. (Glenn had observed the particles after each sunrise on his orbital flights.)

The National Aeronautics and Space Administration is seriously considering Dr. Herzfeld's suggestion that a spectroscope be included in the equipment of the next astronaut. A spectrographic reading by the astronaut would show whether the luminosity is in the spectrum for trapped nitrogen and oxygen.

* Science News Letter, 82:23 July 14, 1962
