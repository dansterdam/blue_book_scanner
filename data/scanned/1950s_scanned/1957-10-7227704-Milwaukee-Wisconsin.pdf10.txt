One important phase of the IGY program
will be concerned with the ionosphere, the layer
of the atmosphere that reflects radio waves
back to the earth. This is affected by the
sun's radiation. When the moon cuts this off,
important observations can be made.

Without traveling to Antarctica, anyone
who can see the evening skies in October
will be able to see another eclipse—not once
but several times. This will be of the star
Algol, or in the constellation of Perseus,
the champion, which is seen in the north-
east, just about midnight.

Algol, also known as beta Persei, is the
second brightest star in this constellation:
the brightest is Mirfak, a little to the left.
Ordinarily, Algol is of magnitude 2.08,
while Mirfak is 1.50, or about 25% brighter.
Below Algol (under the letter
Perseus) is the star called epsilon Persei, of
magnitude 2.88, and Algol is about twice
as bright.

However, if you look at these stars at
about 9:07 p.m. on the evening of Oct. 18,
you will find that their order of brightness
has changed. Both epsilon and epsilon is about 50%
brighter than Algol, which is now only a
third as bright as it was previously. On the
evenings of Oct. 19 and 20, Algol will
shine with its normal brightness. Then on
the 21st, 2.82 days after its previous diminu-
tion in brightness, it will again have faded.

Actually, Algol is not a single orb, but
consists of two stars revolving around the
center of gravity of the pair. There are
many binary stars of which this is true,
not with such regularity in the phase of
evolution is nearly in line with the earth,
and one star is much fainter than the
other.

Thus, over 2 days 20 hours 48 minutes,
the dark component of Algol passes par-
tially in front of the bright one and pro-
duces an eclipse, which dims its light. It
takes about ten hours for the complete
passage of the dark star.
